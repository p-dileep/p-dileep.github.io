Recognising a radio signal without being told what it is

PhD, Department of Electronics and Electrical Engineering, Indian Institute of Technology Guwahati, 2026.

The problem

Every radio transmitter packs its data onto a carrier wave in a particular pattern, called a modulation. A receiver normally knows the pattern in advance. Sometimes it does not: a radio that shares spectrum with others, or a monitoring station, has to work out the pattern from the signal alone. That task is automatic modulation classification.

What I did

My thesis, Automatic Modulation Classification using Shallow CNNs, asks how small a neural network can be and still do this well. A small network matters because it has to run inside a radio, not in a data centre. The networks read the raw received samples directly, with no hand-crafted features.

The work has three parts:

  • One antenna. Compact convolutional networks that use dropout in place of the usual pooling layers, tested on signals that have passed through fading channels.
  • Several receivers working together. When individual receivers each hear a weak copy of the signal, combining what they hear recovers accuracy that no single one can reach.
  • Many antennas. Classification for multi-antenna (MIMO) links, including correlated channels and keyhole channels, where every path is squeezed through a single narrow opening.

Supervisors: Dr. A. Rajesh and Prof. P. K. Bora.
